Your project in one sentence:

> We model epidemic spread across the OpenFlights airport network and compare which airport-closure strategy controls the outbreak best under limited closure budgets.

Memorize this flow:

1. Data/network  
   - OpenFlights dataset.
   - Airports = nodes; flight routes = undirected edges.
   - Largest connected component: 3,188 airports and 18,833 routes.

2. Main question  
   - If only 1–20% of airports can be closed, which airports should be chosen to reduce an epidemic most effectively?

3. Strategies compared  
   - Random closure: baseline.
   - Degree: airports with the most direct connections.
   - Closeness: airports that are, on average, nearest to all others.
   - Betweenness: airports that lie on many shortest paths.
   - Eigenvector: airports connected to other influential airports.

4. Epidemic model  
   - SIR: Susceptible → Infected → Recovered.
   - Infection rate: \(\beta=0.30\); recovery rate: \(\gamma=0.10\).
   - Mean-field threshold: \(R_0=\beta/\gamma\), so here \(R_0=3>1\): an outbreak can grow.
   - The network model also allows infection/population movement along routes.

5. Fair comparison  
   - Every strategy starts from the same high-degree infected airport.
   - That airport is protected from closure; otherwise a strategy could “win” just by removing the initial infection source.

6. Metrics  
   - Final outbreak size.
   - Peak infected fraction.
   - Time to peak.
   - Largest connected-component fraction: how fragmented the remaining network becomes.

7. Core result  
   - At 20% closure: closeness was best for final outbreak (1.80%), then degree (3.00%).
   - Degree reached the 5% containment target first, at 18%.
   - Random closure was ineffective: about 61.63% final outbreak at 20%.
   - Targeted closure works, but can strongly fragment connectivity.

8. Supporting result  
   - Delaying intervention makes outcomes much worse.
   - Network details matter beyond just the degree distribution, shown by the degree-preserving randomized-network comparison.

9. Limitations  
   - Routes are unweighted.
   - No passenger-volume or real population data.
   - Parameters are scenario values, not estimates for a specific disease.
   - No rerouting/adaptive behavior after closures.

Likely viva questions and short answers:

- “Why use an undirected network?”  
  “It is a simplifying assumption to study structural connectivity. Real flights are directed and weighted, which is a limitation and a natural extension.”

- “Why does closeness perform well?”  
  “It identifies airports with short average paths to the rest of the network, so closing them can reduce efficient global spread.”

- “Why is betweenness not best, despite fragmenting the network?”  
  “Being a bridge for shortest paths is not identical to being most important for this epidemic model. Fragmentation and outbreak suppression are related but different objectives.”

- “Why compare against random closure?”  
  “It gives a fair baseline to show whether centrality-based targeting provides real benefit beyond removing the same number of airports arbitrarily.”

- “Why run five random trials?”  
  “Random closure varies by chance; repeating it makes the comparison less dependent on one lucky or unlucky sample.”

- “What is your main conclusion?”  
  “With limited intervention budgets, targeted centrality-based closure greatly outperforms random closure, but the best strategy depends on whether epidemic control or preserving connectivity is the priority.”
